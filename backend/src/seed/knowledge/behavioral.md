# Behavioral Interviews

## What Interviewers Assess

Behavioral questions assess how you worked in real situations: ownership, collaboration, communication, handling conflict and failure, learning ability, prioritisation and impact. Interviewers look for specific past examples rather than hypothetical answers, and for your individual contribution within a team.

## The STAR Method

Structure answers with STAR:

- Situation: brief context — the project, team and constraint (one or two sentences).
- Task: your responsibility or the goal.
- Action: what you specifically did, the decisions you made and why. This should be the longest part. Use "I" for your actions while crediting the team.
- Result: the measurable outcome (latency reduced by 40%, feature shipped two weeks early, bug rate dropped) and what you learned.

A strong answer takes about two minutes, stays on one story, quantifies results where possible and ends with a reflection.

## Common Questions

- Tell me about yourself: a 60-90 second narrative — current focus, one or two highlights relevant to the role, and why this role.
- Describe a challenging project: pick one with technical depth where you made meaningful decisions.
- Tell me about a conflict with a teammate: show empathy, focus on the issue rather than the person, describe how you reached alignment and what changed.
- Describe a failure or mistake: take ownership, explain the root cause, the fix and the process change that prevents recurrence. Avoid disguised strengths.
- Tell me about a time you learned something quickly: show your learning approach — documentation, small experiments, asking experts.
- Handling tight deadlines: explain prioritisation, scope negotiation and communication with stakeholders.
- Why this company: connect specific aspects of the product, team or technology to your experience and goals.

## Communication Tips

Pause to pick the right story. Lead with the headline, then details. Avoid jargon the interviewer may not share, and explain acronyms. Be honest about what you did not do. Prepare five to seven stories from projects, internships and coursework that can be adapted to many questions, covering leadership, conflict, failure, impact and learning.

## Talking About Projects

When discussing a project, explain the problem it solves, the architecture and why you chose it, your personal contributions, the hardest technical challenge and how you solved it, trade-offs you made, how you tested it, and what you would improve with more time. Interviewers often ask follow-ups on scalability, security and failure handling.
